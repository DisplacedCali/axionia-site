# Axionia — site review, 27 August 2026

Pass over the public marketing surface only: `app/page.tsx`, `/platform`,
`/deck`, and the components they pull. Written to be worked through, not filed.
Ordered by reversibility rather than by impact — see *How to work this* below.

**Headline:** the site argues well and it argues three times. The home page runs
~1,940 words over eleven sections making essentially one case (*nobody
independently checked this*) at three levels of abstraction: a diagnosis, an
arithmetic, and a set of values. Each is well written; together they queue
rather than compound. The larger miss is that nothing on the home page says
what Axionia is **instead of** — every claim is made against an unnamed
"chain", so a reader already carrying Springbuk, or reading an Artemis Console
scorecard, or three years into a Mercer contract cannot tell whether we replace
them, sit beside them, or duplicate them.

One accuracy problem is separated out as C1 and should be treated as more than
a copy tidy: the site currently asserts clinical overlap between programs,
which is not something we can know without claims data. See below.

---

## How to work this

**One file-coherent change, one commit, one `COMMIT_MSG.txt`.** The natural unit
is a finding, but where several findings touch the same file they ship together
— four separate commits against `app/page.tsx` would need interactive staging,
which the sandbox can't do. Track A is therefore one commit, not four.

**Order by reversibility, not by impact.** Deletions first: "wrong" costs
nothing and the revert is exact. Then relocations, where content moves and
nothing is rewritten. Then merges, which need new prose. Then new components.
The differentiator section (D2) is the biggest miss on the site and is
deliberately last — writing its copy against a page that is about to lose four
sections means writing it twice.

**Mechanical and editorial are different jobs.** Deleting a duplicated sentence
doesn't touch voice. Merging two sections does. Editorial items arrive as
copy-only drafts with no code attached, and become commits after Tom has edited
them. Never more than one editorial item in flight.

**Gates, by owner.** `npx tsc --noEmit` is the agent's and runs in the sandbox.
Reading the diff is Tom's. `npm run build` and looking at the page is Tom's —
the build does not finish inside the sandbox command timeout.

## Track C — editorial

New prose required. Drafted as copy first, edited by Tom, then committed.

**C1 — the site claims overlap it cannot know. It means double-counted value.**
This is an accuracy item, not a copy tidy, and it is upstream of C2.

`BenefitOverlap.tsx` is already correct and its header comment is more precise
than any copy around it. The math is the independent-overlap union of *claims
made against a pool of avoidable spend* —
`dedup = pool × (1 − Π(1 − claimᵢ / pool))` — deliberately assuming programs
overlap only as much as chance would predict, stated as a floor so the number
survives a vendor pushing back. The comment says the defect plainly: *no one
owns the union.*

Everything visible says something else and something we cannot support:

- the component name `BenefitOverlap`
- the eyebrow "The savings nobody de-duplicates"
- the headline "Five programs. Five sets of savings. One population."
- `REVIEW_GAPS[1]` — "whether the fourth one overlaps the first three"

All four assert that the *programs* overlap, which is a clinical claim about
care delivery that we have no data to make and that CLAUDE.md's
never-invent-library-data rule should have caught. The defensible claim is
narrower and harder to argue with: **five attribution models are pointed at one
pool of avoidable spend, so some of that value is being counted more than once,
and nobody owns the union.**

Vocabulary settled 2026-08-27: **double-counted value**. Framing rule carries
over unchanged — nobody is lying, each vendor reports its own program correctly,
and the duplication is a property of owning all five at once. The distinction
to hold in the copy is that vendors are not double-*charging*; the value is
being double-*counted*.

*Fix: rename the component, rewrite the four labels above, and reword
`REVIEW_GAPS[1]` so it describes attribution rather than care.*

**C2 — the stack and the double-count are one section wearing two hats.**
*A dollar here. Six dollars there.* and the double-counting section are the same
population from two sides; the code comment on the second says so. They run as
two full-height sections with ~260 words of setup between them, and they sit on
adjacent `bg-base-2` blocks (`:262`, `:293`) with no rule, so they already merge
visually without the benefit of being edited together. *Fix: one section, two
views, one setup — what you pay, then what you're actually getting.*

**C3 — trim *In plain terms*. Applied 2026-08-27, smaller than specified.**
B3 had already taken the fourth paragraph, and cutting to two would have meant
merging the audit half into the design half — new sentences, and PROJECT_STATE
is explicit that having both in that order is the product.

What was actually wrong was narrower: paragraph three was a trailer for a
section 250 lines below it. It promised "we tell you what else the same money
could buy"; the later section is titled "Knowing what it's worth is the start.
Knowing what else it could buy is the point." It also restated the incumbency
claim that the later pull quote makes better. The restatement is gone and the
promise stays, along with the no-catalogue line, which A1 deliberately kept
here as the place a first-time reader meets it.

## Track F — the population exposure model

Spec at `docs/EXPOSURE_MODEL.md`, drafted 2026-08-27. Nothing built.

The segment library carries labour-market dimensions and no epidemiological
ones, so every population-fit claim on the site currently rests on one opaque
per-industry transfer rate in `ReportDemo` with no derivation and no source.

The spec replaces it with a product of named multipliers against a stated
reference population, each index at 1.00 by default, each traceable to a public
annual source — BLS CPS for age and sex mix, BLS SOII for musculoskeletal and
exposure rates by NAICS, BLS ORS or O*NET work context for chemical and
ergonomic load, BLS shift data for addressability.

Three things in it are worth not losing:

- **Covered lives is an input, not a coefficient.** Disney removed spousal
  coverage for spouses with their own employer's plan on 24 August 2026. A
  segment constant would have been wrong about them four days ago.
- **The model has a known failure direction and says so.** Prevalence and spend
  can move in opposite directions — a manufacturing workforce with more births
  than a law firm that spends more on fertility. A prevalence-only model is
  predictably wrong there, so care-seeking pathway is recorded as unmodelled
  and the example lives in the copy rather than in a coefficient.
- **A falsification test, set before the first engagement.** Segment-adjusted
  predictions must beat a flat all-industry baseline out of sample, or the
  model is decoration and gets deleted rather than tuned.

It also absorbs the fifth taxonomy problem: `ReportDemo`'s four workforce
profiles are unrelated to the library's nine segments. The demo's private
taxonomy gets deleted, not extended.

**Stage 0 was run on 2026-08-31 and it does not work as specified.**

Against MEPS-IC 2020 Table I.C.1, an exposure-based ordering correlates with
published premium at **Spearman rho = −0.70** — significant at n=9, and
backwards. The model is not what failed. Premium measures what an employer
chose to buy, not what its population costs: professional services has the
highest premium and the lowest physical exposure, agriculture the reverse.
Plan generosity tracks compensation, and compensation runs opposite to physical
work.

As written, "failing is disqualifying" would have deleted a working model on
the strength of a confounded outcome. Stage 0 is now a **confound check** —
does the index accidentally measure wage level — with the prediction registered
in advance that |rho| against premium should be *below* 0.4.

No public dataset can validate this model. Every industry-level outcome is
either confounded by plan design or is one of the model's own inputs. The
burden of proof is entirely on Stages 1 and 2, and the spec says so now.

The afternoon was still worth spending: it cost one public table to find a flaw
that would otherwise have surfaced as a wrong verdict after a build cycle.

Granularity, category coverage, the prevalence/severity split and the
falsification thresholds are all settled in the spec as of 2026-08-27. It is
ready to be built or argued with, and nothing in it is open.

### The headline

The site sells the audit. The documents sell the design.

The site's argument is that a vendor claims savings, we check whether it is
true, and then we tell you what else the money could buy. Vendor auditing is
the hero and the generative half is the sequel.

The documents' argument is that the largest money is in the **tax shape of the
dollar** and has nothing to do with vendors. Finding 01 is $492,000 a year
available at no extra cost. The vendor-claim teardown is finding 08 of 9 and is
not the largest. The efficiency frontier states it outright: *$117,052
available at your current spend, no new budget; 81¢ to 88¢ delivered per dollar
at identical cost.*

The assessment's own sentence is the positioning the site does not have:

> That is a compensation argument, not a benefits argument, and it is the
> version a CFO can approve.

The site says the CFO cannot get in the room. The documents hand the CFO a
reallocation needing no new budget — an easier yes and a much larger number.

### A fourth framework, and it is better than the three we argued about

E1 spent this week on three sets of eight axes. **The documents use none of
them.** They use Findings → Recommendations with confidence → Open decisions
with break-evens, and underneath, *How we decide: four tests, in order — Tax,
Reach, Evidence, Trade — and one override.*

The override is the part no scoring radar has: *does it protect against
something they cannot self-insure? If yes, poor scores on the four tests are
acceptable.* Without it the framework recommends deleting life and disability
cover, which the document says plainly.

**This should probably be the public methodology, with any axis score as an
output rather than the frame.** That reopens E1 in a better direction than the
one it was paused in.

### Five devices to move onto the site, in order of value

1. **Evidence tags.** `STRONG PROOF` / `WEAK PROOF` / `NO PROOF` /
   `VENDOR-BLESSED` / `THIN` / `SETTLED LAW`. The site *claims* transparency;
   the documents make it a visible grammar on every number. Most transferable
   thing in either file.
2. **The 90-second denominator test.** Check whether a claim's own numbers are
   possible before arguing about clinical evidence. $2,941 PMPY becomes
   $338,215 becomes 29.4% of plan spend against a category worth 13% —
   impossible as stated — and the vendor's own SEC filing discloses 3.4% active
   users, so $11,499, or 1%. Self-contained, teachable, and the most shareable
   thing written here. **Deserves its own page.**
3. **Break-evens instead of estimates.** "Stop 1.1 of 18 expected departures a
   year" is judgeable. "The gym might help retention" is not.
4. **Report grade** — client-specific versus built on population defaults.
   Nobody else grades their own deliverable's inputs.
5. **"What we would keep" and "What we are not recommending, and why."**
   PROJECT_STATE already records the site running 20:1 diagnostic against
   generative. These documents defend existing benefits explicitly, which is
   what makes them read as an advisor rather than an auditor.

### Three places the documents contradict the site

**Scale.** The site targets $7–12M mid-market and Northrock is 1,450 people.
These model **114 people and $1.88M**, and the analysis is *better* at that
size — discrimination testing and the credibility maths (16% at 114 lives, 6%
at 14) bite hardest for small employers. The site currently tells that buyer
they are too small. They may be the best fit.

**Effort.** The site promises no software, no data feed, "you tell us what you
are running". The engagement runs census, plan documents, vendor contracts and
funding, **plus two hours of interview for "the six things a form cannot ask"**
— and the interview is where the value comes from. Underselling it undersells
the product.

**Subject matter.** 401(k) match, HSA, FSA, §127 student loan repayment,
discrimination testing, funding structure, cafeteria plan routing. That is most
of what the documents do and **none of it appears anywhere on the public
site.**

### The case study

There is now something stronger than Northrock: **Axionia ran it on itself and
published the numbers.** "We did this to our own company first" is a trust move
nobody in the E1 landscape makes. Northrock stays as the employer-like-you

## Outbound assets — built against the Minnesota target list

`axionia_mn_target_list.xlsx` was read on 2026-08-31 and it changed two
decisions that were about to be made the other way.

**608 targets.** Minnesota, self-funded, 250–5,000 participants, from three
years of DOL Form 5500 filings. 523,188 covered lives, ~$6.28B estimated
medical spend. Tiers A/B/C at 60/160/388. Median 541 participants; 281 of 608
sit in the 250–500 band. Manufacturing is 165 employers and $1.7B, more than
twice the next sector.

**The self-assessment is the wrong case study for this list.** It models 114
people. The list's *minimum* is 250 and its median is 541 — every target is
larger than the company in that document. Northrock at 1,450 is inside the band
and is a manufacturer, which is 27% of the list. The self-assessment stays a
warm-intro asset, where "we did this to ourselves" is the point and size is
not.

**The list yields benefits leaders, not CFOs.** 592 of 608 carry a named
individual from the 5500 signature block, but the Method sheet is explicit that
signers are plan administrators — "the benefits owner or a direct report, not
necessarily the economic buyer." So the denominator test is the right first
asset and the CFO reallocation argument needs a different route.

**The disclosure policy gives the opener and forbids the creepy one.** Broker of
record, compensation, change history, score and tier are internal-only and must
never reach a prospect. Client-safe covers participants, funding, carriers and
trust financials — facts about the client's own plan, cited to their own
filing. **That is the personalisation hook: open on a fact from their own Form
5500.** Public record, their own document, and almost nobody does it.

### `/check-a-claim` — built 2026-08-31

The ninety-second test as a tool the reader runs on their own numbers. Four
inputs, all supplied by the visitor: covered lives, annual plan spend, the
claimed per-member saving, and the share who actually use it.

**The page holds no data of our own.** Not one benchmark, category share or
engagement assumption. That is deliberate and it is why it is hard to argue
with — the arithmetic does the work. It also means it needed no invented
constants, which is the CLAUDE.md rule that killed several easier designs.

**No vendor is named.** The worked example is real — a published $2,941 PMPY
claim and that company's own SEC-disclosed 3.4% active-user rate — but naming
it turns a method into an attack, invites a letter, and contradicts the
position on `/where-we-fit` that everyone in this market is good at something.

Brand: the vendor's figure is Amber throughout, per the semantic scale — their
claim, unadjusted, not wrong but unverified. Nothing is Risk red, because a
claim that fails this test is usually a denominator mismatch rather than a lie,
and the verdict copy says so.

### Two housekeeping items on the list itself

- **36 targets last filed in 2023.** Stale; confirm the plan still exists
  before outreach.
- **The `Broker history` column is measuring the wrong thing. Resolved
  2026-08-31 — do not target on it.**

  Brown & Brown showed 51 of 53 accounts "changed". Investigated, and the firm
  files under **21 distinct spellings** across its 58 accounts — `BROWN & BROWN
  INSURANCE SERVICES`, `BROWN & BROWN INS SERVICES, INC.`, `BROWN & BROWN INS.
  SERVICES, INC.` and eighteen more, differing by a comma, an abbreviation or a
  full stop. Same firm, typed differently from one filing year to the next.

  The pattern holds across the whole list. Change rate scales with **years
  observed**, not with anything about the account:

  | Years observed | Accounts | Flagged changed |
  |---|---:|---:|
  | 1 | 49 | 0% |
  | 2 | 217 | 36% |
  | 3 | 263 | 50% |

  More filing years means more chances for the string to differ. Genuine
  broker-of-record churn runs roughly 10–15% a year, so three years should
  yield 25–30%, not 50% — and no broker loses 100% of fifty accounts in two
  years.

  **The flag detects string inequality between years, not broker changes.** The
  211 figure is substantially inflated and the "recently changed broker = in
  play" segment cannot be used for targeting until it is rebuilt.

  *The fix is in the build script, not the workbook.* Broker of record per year
  lives in `F_SCH_A_PART1_YYYY_latest.csv`, keyed on EIN + plan number, already
  listed as a source on the Method sheet. Normalise before comparing years:
  uppercase, strip punctuation, collapse `&`/`AND`, drop `INC` `LLC` `INS`
  `INSURANCE` `SERVICES` `SVCS` `AGENCY` `OF MINNESOTA`. A rough normaliser
  collapsed all 21 Brown & Brown strings to one; a dozen lines would do it.

  Two things to do alongside: add this to **Known Limitations** on the Method
  sheet, which already carries the stop-loss and broker-comp caveats honestly
  and where this belongs; and note that **132 of 608 have no usable signal
  anyway** (`Insufficient history` 53, `None` 79), so even corrected the change
  signal covers about three-quarters of the list.

  **Target on the other signals meanwhile** — Tier A, sector, size band, and
  especially the **53 employers adding carriers**, which is point-solution
  accumulation, is unaffected by this bug, and is arguably the better segment
  for `/check-a-claim` anyway: someone who just added a vendor has a fresh claim
  on their desk.
